## The idea

Politicians can use AI to write speeches and official documents, and nobody has
to say so. We wanted a tool that tells you roughly how much of a text a machine
wrote, as a single number like "28% AI", instead of a flat HUMAN or AI label.
Most writing is a mix, and one label hides that.

## Making our own data

The plan was to test on real speeches. Problem: nearly all of them were written
by people, so there was nothing to test against. So we made a dataset where we
knew exactly how much AI was in each record.

We took 200 public appointment records from the
[Investigative Journalism Foundation](https://theijf.org/appointments): 100
Manitoba Orders in Council and 100 federal Privy Council appointments (half
French). A Python pipeline sent each one to GPT-4 with a target for how much to
rewrite, measured the result, and retried until it hit the target.

| Set        | Records | What we did     | Text kept |
| ---------- | ------- | --------------- | --------- |
| Provincial | 40%     | Full AI rewrite | 24%       |
| Provincial | 40%     | Light edit      | 97%       |
| Provincial | 20%     | Left alone      | 100%      |
| Federal    | 60%     | Moderate edit   | 82%       |
| Federal    | 40%     | Deeper rewrite  | 64%       |

The fiddly part was keeping every name, date, dollar amount and legal citation
intact. Legal text is full of full stops that don't end sentences (`c. P271`,
`$4,404.38`), and 19 records didn't follow the usual layout, so the pipeline
detects the language of each line instead of trusting fixed phrases. In the end
it ran all 200 with zero failures, about 40 seconds per 100.

## What we built

- **Live detector.** Speak into the mic and it transcribes you, then colours each
  sentence by how AI it reads, every 80 words or 2.5 second pause.
- **Hacker badge.** Joshua got the Hack the North badge running and wrote a
  small API for it in Lua, so our Next.js app can hit it directly and change
  what the badge shows. It sits on the same WiFi and flashes red when a section
  is 50%+ AI, green when it isn't.
- **Analyze.** Paste a YouTube or [CPAC](https://www.cpac.ca/search) link with a timestamp and it scores 60
  seconds of audio.
- **Database and Map.** All 205 records, searchable, and plotted in 2D/3D by
  meaning so you can spot near-identical text that scored very differently.

## Tech Stack

Built with [GPTZero](https://gptzero.me/) for AI detection, [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/) Scribe for live transcription,
Elasticsearch kNN with Jina embeddings for the map, Next.js, SQLite and yt-dlp + ffmpeg for videos.

## What we found

Across the 200 rewritten records, the average AI share was 35%. 81 records
scored 50% or more, and 74 scored under 10%.

<ShareHistogram />

The federal records, which all got edited, mostly land around 60–70%. The
provincial ones pile up near zero, even though 40 of them were full rewrites.
Only about 20 provincial records scored above 50%. **That gap is on us: some of
the rewritten data got missed during processing, so not every full rewrite made
it into the scored set.**

The five real speeches we tried (Stephen Harper, Evan Solomon, the EU State of
the Union, and a couple of others) all read as human sentence by sentence,
between 0% and 8%.

## The Bill Oliver clip

One of those real clips is a [New Brunswick MLA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kaJgX_FIcsw) who read a chatbot's reply out
loud in the legislature, preamble and all. Score it as one block and you get
28% AI. Score it sentence by sentence and average, and it drops to 8%. The line
that literally is the chatbot talking? 6%.

## What we learned

AI detectors aren't great with speech. Short clips don't give them enough to go
on, so they often miss AI text, which means a "human" result on a short clip
doesn't prove much. And the same text can score very differently depending on
how you feed it in, like the Bill Oliver clip above. So we'd treat a detector
score as a hint, not a verdict.
